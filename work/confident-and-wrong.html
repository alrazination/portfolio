Confident and wrong

A phishing awareness check that asks people how sure they are. The riskiest employees aren't the ones who fail. They're the ones who get it wrong without any doubt, because they'll click without hesitating and never report it.

Format
Confidence-based check with xAPI reporting
Audience
All staff, run twice a year
My role
Evaluation design, writing, build, data model
Built with
HTML, CSS, JavaScript, xAPI

Concept project. The brief, the company (Kestrel & Finch) and the messages are invented. The results you see in the demo are your own answers, calculated in your browser and not sent anywhere.

Check five messages

You work at Kestrel & Finch. Your manager is Lena Brandt. For each message, decide whether it's phishing and how sure you are. You'll see the answers at the end.

The brief

The security team runs a yearly phishing quiz. The average score is above 90%. Their simulated phishing campaigns still get clicks, and very few staff report suspicious emails.

The quiz says people know what phishing looks like. Behaviour says otherwise. The question was: what is the quiz failing to measure?

The insight

A right answer can be a guess, and a wrong answer can be held with total confidence. A standard quiz treats both the same. The four combinations mean very different things:

  • Sure and right: reliable. Leave them alone.
  • Unsure and right: on the way. They need practice, not more content.
  • Unsure and wrong: they know they don't know, so they're likely to ask.
  • Sure and wrong: the real risk. They act without checking, and won't think to report.

This draws on certainty-based marking, developed by Tony Gardner-Medwin at UCL, where learners rate their confidence in each answer. I used it here for evaluation, not grading: no one is penalised for honest uncertainty.

Design decisions

Confidence before the answer is revealed

Learners rate their confidence on every message before seeing any feedback. Otherwise early feedback changes how they rate later ones.

Legitimate messages are in the mix

Two of five are genuine. Training that only shows phishing teaches people to distrust everything, which slows work and buries real reports in noise.

The debrief teaches the cue, not just the answer

Each message's explanation names the one cue that gives it away: a lookalike domain, a personal address, a request to bypass normal process.

Results go to teams, not to names

Reporting groups the sure-and-wrong rate by team, so security can target follow-up without anyone being named and shamed. That keeps people honest about their confidence.

The data model

Each answer is sent as an xAPI statement to a learning record store, with confidence as an extension. That's what makes the four-quadrant reporting possible in any LRS dashboard.
