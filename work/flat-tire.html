Changing a flat tire

Where it started

A tire change is usually taught as a numbered list: brake, loosen the nuts, jack up, swap. Lists are easy to remember and easy to get wrong, because nothing in a list tells you why step two comes before step three.

Each step is there because of something physical. A car on a jack can roll. A wheel in the air spins when you turn the nuts. If learners see that happen, they don't need to memorise the order. They can work it out.

The core mechanic

Learners drag real tools onto the part of the car they act on. The simulation doesn't stop them from doing things in the wrong order. It plays out what would happen.

What the learner doesWhat happens
Jack up the car before setting the parking brake
→
The jack topples, the car rolls forward and drops. Critical danger.
Loosen the nuts with the car in the air
→
The wheel spins with the wrench. Without ground friction, the nuts won't break loose.
Fit the spare before lifting or loosening
→
Not enough clearance, or the nuts are still tight. The reason is spelled out.
Do it in order
→
Each step is ticked off on the procedure tracker, the car is lowered, and you're ready to drive.

Designing the mistakes

Design decisions

Let the wrong order happen

Blocking a wrong move teaches nothing. Playing it out shows the reason for the rule.

Match the consequence to the risk

A minor slip gets a short message. A dangerous one gets a red alert and a reset.

Put the physics in the feedback

Messages name the cause, like ground friction or clearance, not just "wrong order".

A tracker, not a checklist

The procedure tracker fills in as steps are done, so learners build the list themselves instead of reading it first.
