From 38 slides to 12 minutes of practice

A data protection onboarding deck, rebuilt around the six decisions new staff actually face. The law is still there, but it shows up as feedback on a choice instead of a wall of text before it.

Format
Scenario-based eLearning, 12 minutes, mobile-first
Audience
New staff in customer-facing roles
My role
Audit, action mapping, writing, build
Built with
HTML, CSS, JavaScript

Concept project. I wrote a typical 38-slide onboarding deck as the starting point, modelled on the ones many companies use, then rebuilt it. No client material or data was used. This isn't legal advice: a real version would be reviewed by the client's data protection officer.

Play four of the six decisions

You've just started in customer service at a delivery company. No slides first. Just your first week.

The brief

A delivery company onboards every new hire with a 38-slide data protection deck and a ten-question quiz. Almost everyone passes. Customer data still ends up in personal inboxes, on USB sticks and with the wrong recipient, and mistakes get reported late or not at all.

What the deck was doing

I sorted every slide by what it asks of the learner. Most of the deck explained the law. Very little of it helped someone decide what to do with a real request on a Tuesday afternoon.

Before: 38 slides

  • 21 on the regulation, its history and principles
  • 9 definitions
  • 6 on what to actually do
  • 2 with contacts
  • Then a recall quiz

After: 6 decisions

  • Each one starts with a situation from the job
  • Feedback explains the rule at the moment it matters
  • Definitions appear only where a decision needs them
  • A one-page summary to keep at the end
  • The decisions are the assessment

Action map

Using Cathy Moore's action mapping, I started from the goal and listed what staff must do, not what they must know. Anything that didn't support one of these actions moved to the reference sheet or was cut.

GoalFewer data incidents, and every incident reported on the same day.
RecogniseSpot personal data even when there's no name on it.
VerifyConfirm who you're talking to before sharing anything.
Keep it inUse only approved tools, even when they're inconvenient.
ReportTell the data protection contact straight away when something goes wrong.
RefuseSay no to colleagues asking for data without a work reason.
DeleteFollow the retention schedule instead of keeping files "just in case".

Design decisions

Decision first, rule second

Each screen opens with a situation and asks what you'd do. The principle behind it appears in the feedback, attached to the choice you just made.

People remember a rule better when it explains something they just did than when they read it in advance.

The tempting answer is always there

Emailing a file home to finish it is the helpful-employee move. That's why it's an option, and why the feedback acknowledges the good intention before explaining the risk.

Training that pretends the wrong choice is obviously wrong doesn't prepare anyone for the moment it feels right.

Reporting is framed as the right move, not a confession

The mistake scenario rewards speed of reporting and makes clear that the company may have as little as 72 hours to notify the regulator.

Late reporting is usually about fear, not ignorance. The design has to make reporting feel safe.

Built for a phone, in 12 minutes

One decision per screen, large tap targets, no hover-dependent interactions. New hires often do onboarding between shadowing shifts.

How I'd measure it

WhatHowWhen
DecisionsFirst-attempt choices per scenario, to see which situations are still misunderstoodIn the module
Reporting speedHours between an incident and its report to the data protection contactMonthly, against the prior year
IncidentsMisdirected emails and unapproved transfers flagged by ITQuarterly
Time costMedian time in the module versus the old deck and quizAt launch

One thing to warn the client about: reported incidents may go up at first. That's usually good news, because mistakes that were hidden are now visible.
