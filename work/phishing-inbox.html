Where it started

Security awareness training has a reputation problem. People sit through it once a year, click through the slides, and pass a quiz that asks them to spot a phishing email they’ve been told is there.

Nobody falls for phishing because they don’t know it exists. They fall for it on a busy Monday, between real emails, when one looks routine. And they never see what happens next, because the damage happens on servers they can’t see.

The core mechanic

Learners work through an inbox with real-looking senders, links that work and attachments that open. Three of the eight emails are genuine, so reporting everything doesn’t win. Next to the inbox, a live map of the office network shows what each decision does.

Designing the mistakes

Design decisions

Make the hidden visible

The status bar shows where every link really goes, as a real mail client does. The habit being trained is pointing before clicking.

Not reporting has a cost

Leaving a fake in the inbox lets a colleague click it. Reporting protects other people, and the simulation shows exactly who.

Real emails in the mix

Three genuine emails stop learners from winning by reporting everything. Judgment, not suspicion, is the skill.

Name the red flags on the email

After every decision the clues are numbered on the message itself: the domain, the pressure, the link. Learners see where to look next time.
