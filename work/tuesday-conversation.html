The Tuesday conversation

New team leads put off difficult conversations for weeks, then have them badly. This simulation lets them practise one before it's real, and shows how each choice affects both the relationship and the outcome.

Format
Branching simulation, 5 minutes
Audience
Newly promoted team leads
My role
Research, design, writing, build
Built with
HTML, CSS, JavaScript

Concept project. The brief is self-initiated and the company, people and dialogue are invented to show my process. There's no client data and no results yet, so you'll find a plan for measuring it instead.

Play it first

You're a new team lead. Jonas, until recently your peer, has missed the last three Friday report deadlines. You have a 15-minute one-to-one.

The brief

A growing company promotes most of its team leads from within. Six months in, HR hears the same thing: new leads avoid giving feedback, especially to former peers, until a small problem becomes a formal one.

The existing training is a 40-minute video on feedback models. Completion is high. Leads can name the model. They still don't have the conversation.

The real problem

This isn't a knowledge gap. A new lead knows they should be specific and listen. What they lack is the experience of saying it out loud to someone they had lunch with last month, and seeing that it can go well.

So I mapped the conversation as a few moments where leads commonly go wrong, using Cathy Moore's action mapping to keep only what supports those moments:

Business goalPerformance issues are raised within two weeks, not left for months.
OpeningName the specific issue and invite their view, without softening it into small talk or turning it into a verdict.
PushbackStay on the issue when the other person deflects, without getting defensive.
CauseFind out what's getting in the way before proposing a fix.
CloseAgree who does what by when, and when you'll check in.

Design decisions

Two meters instead of a score

Every choice moves both trust and clarity. Real conversations trade one against the other: being blunt is clear but costly, being warm can be vague.

A single score teaches "find the right answer". Two meters teach the trade-off a lead is actually making.

Wrong options are things real leads say

No option is obviously silly. "Let's both keep an eye on it" and "I'll take the reports off your plate" feel kind in the moment. That's exactly why people choose them.

If distractors are easy to spot, learners practise spotting distractors, not judgment.

Jonas reacts before the coach comments

The next scene opens with Jonas's response to what you said, so the consequence shows up in the conversation itself. The coaching note explains why.

Seeing someone shut down teaches more than being told "that was too direct".

Short enough to replay

Three decisions, under five minutes. The debrief shows the path you took and nudges you to try a different one.

Most of the learning happens on the second run, when people test the option they were curious about.

Decision map

How I'd measure it

Completion would tell us nothing here, so the plan looks at whether conversations happen sooner and go better, using Will Thalheimer's LTEM tiers as a guide.

WhatHowWhen
Decision-makingShare of leads who reach "clear plan" on their first run, and on their secondIn the module
ConfidenceTwo questions before and after: "How likely are you to raise an issue this week?"In the module
TransferLeads log their next real feedback conversation in a two-minute form: when, how it wentWithin 4 weeks
Business effectTime from a problem first being noticed to being raised, from HR case notes3 and 6 months

What I'd do next

With a real client, the dialogue would come from interviews with a few experienced leads and the people they manage. The best lines in scenarios are always the ones someone actually said.

I'd then build two more conversations with different causes behind the same symptom (a skills gap, a motivation problem), so leads learn to diagnose rather than memorise one script.
